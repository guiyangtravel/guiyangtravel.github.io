The Ultimate Guiyang Travel Itinerary: 3-5 Days of Adventure

  1. Home
  2. Travel Blog
  3. Blog Details

If you think Guiyang is just a pit stop on the way to Guilin or a sleepy provincial capital lost in the mist, you are missing out on one of China’s most underrated adventure hubs. Nestled in the heart of Guizhou province, Guiyang is a city that defies expectations. It is a place where karst mountains rise like ancient sentinels from the urban sprawl, where the air smells of spicy sour fish and wet earth, and where every alley hides a story. Over the past few years, Guiyang has quietly become a hotspot for digital nomads, foodies, and outdoor enthusiasts. The city’s “Cool Guiyang” branding is not just marketing fluff—summer temperatures rarely break 30°C, making it a perfect escape from the heatwaves plaguing much of China. But beyond the weather, Guiyang is a launchpad for some of the most jaw-dropping natural wonders in the country. Whether you have three days or five, this itinerary will take you from the neon-lit streets of downtown to the misty depths of ancient caves and the terraced rice paddies of ethnic minority villages. Buckle up, because this is not your grandma’s tour.

Why Guiyang Now? The “Cool Capital” Phenomenon

Let’s talk about the elephant in the room: why is everyone suddenly talking about Guiyang? The answer is a perfect storm of factors. First, the pandemic-era travel restrictions pushed domestic Chinese tourists to explore lesser-known destinations, and Guizhou province, with its low population density and abundant green spaces, became a refuge. Second, the Chinese government has been pouring money into infrastructure here. The high-speed rail network now connects Guiyang to major cities like Chengdu, Guangzhou, and Kunming in under three hours, making it an easy weekend getaway. Third, and most importantly, the local government has aggressively promoted “cool tourism” (清凉旅游). You will see banners everywhere: “Guiyang, the Cool Capital of China.” It works. When I visited in July, my friends in Shanghai were melting at 40°C, while I was wearing a light jacket in the evening.

But the real draw is the fusion of urban and wild. Guiyang is one of the few cities in China where you can hike a forested mountain in the morning, eat a bowl of fiery sour soup noodles for lunch, visit a 500-year-old Ming dynasty temple in the afternoon, and end the night at a craft beer bar run by a former Wall Street trader. The city has a gritty, unpolished charm that feels authentic in a way that Shanghai’s slick modernity or Beijing’s imperial grandeur does not. It is a city that rewards the curious traveler who is willing to wander.

Day 1: Urban Immersion and the Taste of Sour

Morning: Qianling Park and the Monkey Encounter

Start your first day early. I know, you are on vacation, but trust me, the morning mist in Guiyang is something else. Head straight to Qianling Park (黔灵公园), the city’s green lung and a favorite among locals. The park is massive—it covers over 400 hectares—and it is built around a mountain called Qianling Mountain. The entrance fee is a pittance (around 5 RMB), and the views from the top are worth every step. But the real attraction here is the monkeys. And I do not mean a few shy macaques in the trees. I mean hundreds of semi-wild rhesus macaques that have basically taken over the park. They are bold, they are cheeky, and they will absolutely steal your water bottle if you are not careful. Locals bring bags of peanuts and corn to feed them, and the monkeys will climb on your shoulders, grab your hat, and pose for selfies like they are influencers.

Pro tip: Do not bring any shiny objects or open food bags. I saw a tourist lose his sunglasses to a particularly aggressive monkey who then proceeded to chew on them while staring him down. Also, the hike to the top takes about 45 minutes, but there is a cable car if you are feeling lazy. At the summit, you will find Hongfu Temple, a Buddhist temple dating back to the Qing dynasty. The temple is modest but the incense smoke mingling with the mountain mist creates a serene atmosphere. Take a moment to sit on the stone benches and watch the city sprawl below you. It is a reminder that Guiyang is a city of layers—ancient and modern, chaotic and calm.

Late Morning: The Sour Fish Awakening

After descending from the mountain, you will be hungry. And there is only one dish you should eat for your first meal in Guiyang: Suantang Yu (酸汤鱼), or sour soup fish. This is the signature dish of Guizhou cuisine, and it is a revelation. The broth is made from fermented tomatoes and rice, giving it a tangy, slightly spicy flavor that is unlike anything you have tasted. The fish is usually a local river fish, cooked whole in the bubbling pot, and served with a dipping sauce of chili, garlic, and cilantro. The best place to try it is a restaurant called Lao Kaili Suantang Yu (老凯里酸汤鱼) near the city center. It is a bit touristy, but the quality is consistent. Order a side of sticky rice and some wild vegetables stir-fried with garlic. The combination of sour, spicy, and umami will reset your palate.

Afternoon: The Hidden Alleyways of the Old City

Guiyang’s old city is a maze of narrow alleys and crumbling buildings that somehow feel alive. Spend your afternoon wandering the area around Jiaxiu Tower (甲秀楼), a three-story pavilion built on a stone bridge over the Nanming River. The tower is the city’s most iconic landmark, and it is especially beautiful at sunset when the lights come on. But do not just take a photo and leave. Walk across the bridge and explore the small streets behind it. You will find old men playing Chinese chess under banyan trees, women selling handmade embroidery, and tiny shops selling everything from dried mushrooms to antique teapots. This is the real Guiyang, untouched by gentrification.

If you have time, visit the Guizhou Provincial Museum. It is not world-class, but it offers a solid introduction to the province’s ethnic diversity. Guizhou is home to 49 different ethnic groups, including the Miao, Dong, and Buyi, and the museum’s exhibits on their textiles, silver jewelry, and architecture are fascinating. The building itself is a modern architectural statement, shaped like a traditional Dong drum tower.

Evening: The Night Market Crawl

As the sun sets, Guiyang transforms. The sidewalks fill with street vendors selling grilled skewers, stinky tofu, and a local specialty called Siwawa (丝娃娃), which literally translates to “silk baby.” Don’t let the name scare you. Siwawa is a DIY wrap made with a thin rice pancake filled with shredded vegetables, peanuts, and a drizzle of sour-spicy sauce. It is fresh, crunchy, and addictive. Head to Erqi Road Night Market (二七路小吃街) for the best selection. This is a pedestrian-only street lined with stalls that stretch for blocks. Try everything: the grilled black goat meat, the fried rice cakes with fermented bean curd, and the cold noodles with sesame paste. Wash it all down with a bottle of local Guizhou beer or a cup of sour plum juice.

Day 2: Caves, Waterfalls, and the Power of Nature

Full Day Excursion: Huangguoshu Waterfall and Tianxingqiao

On day two, leave the city behind. Guiyang is surrounded by some of the most dramatic landscapes in China, and the crown jewel is Huangguoshu Waterfall (黄果树瀑布), about a two-hour drive southwest. This is the largest waterfall in Asia, and one of the most powerful in the world. During the rainy season (June to August), the water volume is so immense that the spray can be seen from kilometers away. The falls are 77.8 meters high and 101 meters wide, and you can walk behind them through a natural cave called Shuiliandong (Water Curtain Cave). Standing behind a curtain of falling water, feeling the mist on your face while the roar drowns out all other sounds, is a primal experience.

But Huangguoshu is just one part of a larger scenic area. Do not skip Tianxingqiao (天星桥), which is often overshadowed by the main waterfall but is equally stunning. Tianxingqiao is a bizarre landscape of karst pinnacles, underground rivers, and natural stone bridges. You will walk through a forest of jagged rocks that look like they belong on another planet. There is a section called “The Silver Chain Waterfall” where the water cascades down a series of stone steps, creating a shimmering curtain. The entire area is less crowded than the main waterfall, so you can take your time and soak in the atmosphere.

Lunch: Local Farmhouse Cuisine

Between the two attractions, stop for lunch at a farmhouse restaurant. These are simple places run by local families, and the food is honest and delicious. Look for a place that serves “Longli” (龙里) style pork belly, slow-cooked with preserved vegetables and chili. It is fatty, savory, and pairs perfectly with a bowl of plain rice. Also try the local wild mushrooms, which are foraged from the surrounding forests. They have an earthy, meaty flavor that you will not find in city restaurants.

Late Afternoon: The Return to Guiyang

By late afternoon, you will be exhausted but exhilarated. The drive back to Guiyang takes about two hours, and you will likely fall asleep in the car. Once back in the city, do something low-key. Grab a foot massage at one of the many massage parlors near the city center. A 60-minute foot massage costs about 80 RMB, and it will revive your tired legs. Then, head to a local hotpot restaurant for dinner. Guiyang’s hotpot is different from the Sichuan or Chongqing versions. It is lighter, with a broth made from chicken bones and medicinal herbs. The dipping sauce is a mix of crushed peanuts, chili, and fermented tofu. It is comfort food at its finest.

Day 3: Ethnic Villages and the Art of Slow Travel

Morning: The Dong Village of Zhaoxing

If you have three days, this is your final full day, and you should spend it immersing yourself in the culture of Guizhou’s ethnic minorities. The most accessible and impressive village is Zhaoxing Dong Village (肇兴侗寨), about three hours from Guiyang by bus or private car. Zhaoxing is one of the largest Dong villages in China, and it is a UNESCO World Heritage candidate. The village is famous for its five drum towers, each representing a different clan. These towering wooden structures are the heart of the community, used for meetings, festivals, and ceremonies.

Walk through the village and you will see women weaving fabric on hand looms, old men smoking long pipes, and children playing in the cobblestone streets. The architecture is stunning—wooden stilt houses with black tiles and intricate carvings. Do not leave without climbing the small hill behind the village for a panoramic view. The sight of the five drum towers rising above the misty rooftops, with terraced rice paddies stretching into the distance, is one of the most beautiful in China.

Lunch: Dong-Style Glutinous Rice and Sour Pork

In Zhaoxing, eat at a family-run guesthouse. The Dong people are known for their glutinous rice, which is steamed in bamboo tubes and served with sour pork and pickled vegetables. The pork is cured and then fried until crispy, and the rice has a chewy, slightly sweet texture. It is simple food, but it is made with love. Also try the local rice wine, which is cloudy and sweet, with a kick that sneaks up on you.

Afternoon: The Rice Terraces of Tang’an

Just a 20-minute drive from Zhaoxing is the village of Tang’an (堂安), which is famous for its rice terraces. These are not as famous as the Longji terraces in Guangxi, but they are quieter and more authentic. The terraces are carved into the mountainside, and during the summer, they are a vibrant green. You can hike from Tang’an back to Zhaoxing along a trail that winds through the terraces and passes through several smaller villages. The hike takes about two hours and is moderately challenging, but the views are worth it. You will see water buffalo grazing in the paddies, farmers planting rice seedlings, and children waving at you from the path.

Evening: The Grand Song of the Dong People

Back in Zhaoxing, do not miss the evening performance of the “Grand Song of the Dong People” (侗族大歌). This is a polyphonic choral tradition that has been recognized by UNESCO as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The performance takes place in the village square, under the largest drum tower. The singers, usually women in traditional silver headdresses and embroidered costumes, sing without any instrumental accompaniment. Their voices weave together in complex harmonies that sound both ancient and ethereal. It is a moving experience, and it will stay with you long after you leave.

Day 4: The Hidden Gems of Guiyang’s Suburbs

Morning: The Qingyan Ancient Town

If you have four or five days, you can slow down and explore some of Guiyang’s lesser-known attractions. Start day four with a visit to Qingyan Ancient Town (青岩古镇), about 30 kilometers south of the city. Qingyan is a Ming dynasty fortress town that has been remarkably well-preserved. Unlike other ancient towns in China that have been over-commercialized, Qingyan still feels like a living community. The streets are paved with stone, and the buildings are made of dark wood and white plaster. There is a Catholic church, a Buddhist temple, and a Taoist temple all within the same town, a testament to Guizhou’s history of religious tolerance.

The highlight of Qingyan is the “Dingguang Gate,” a massive stone archway that marks the entrance to the old town. Climb the city wall for a view of the surrounding countryside. On a clear day, you can see the karst peaks of the Huaxi River valley. Also try the local snack: Qingyan tofu pudding (青岩豆腐), which is silky, slightly smoky, and served with a spicy soy sauce dip.

Afternoon: The Huaxi River and the National Wetland Park

After Qingyan, head to the Huaxi River (花溪河) area, which is a popular weekend destination for locals. The river is clean and clear, and there are several spots where you can rent a bamboo raft or a paddleboat. But the real attraction is the Huaxi National Wetland Park, a sprawling park with walking trails, lotus ponds, and bird-watching towers. In the summer, the lotus flowers are in full bloom, and the air is filled with the scent of blossoms. This is a great place to relax, read a book, or just watch the world go by.

Evening: The Craft Beer Scene

Guiyang’s nightlife is not as famous as Shanghai’s or Beijing’s, but it has a small but vibrant craft beer scene. The best spot is a bar called “Trip Smith,” located near the city center. The owner is a Chinese-American who studied brewing in the US, and his beers are excellent. Try the “Cool Guiyang” IPA, which is hoppy and citrusy, or the “Mountain Mist” stout, which has notes of coffee and chocolate. The bar has a rooftop terrace with views of the city skyline, and it is a popular hangout for expats and young locals. If you are feeling adventurous, order a plate of “Guiyang-style fried chicken,” which is coated in a spicy Sichuan peppercorn batter.

Day 5: The Grand Finale

Morning: The Miao Village of Xijiang

For your final day, make the trip to Xijiang Qianhu Miao Village (西江千户苗寨), the largest Miao village in China. It is about three hours from Guiyang, but it is worth the journey. Xijiang is a massive, terraced village built on a hillside, with over 1,000 wooden stilt houses. The village is famous for its night view, when thousands of lanterns are lit, creating a sea of twinkling lights. But the daytime is equally impressive. Walk through the narrow alleys, visit the Miao museum, and watch the silver jewelry being made by hand. The Miao people are known for their intricate silverwork, and you can buy earrings, bracelets, and headdresses directly from the artisans.

Lunch: The Miao Long Table Banquet

In Xijiang, you can experience the famous “Long Table Banquet” (长桌宴), a tradition where hundreds of people sit at long tables stretching through the village. The food is served family-style, and includes sour fish, sticky rice, smoked pork, and a local vegetable called “houttuynia” (鱼腥草), which has a strong, earthy flavor that some love and some hate. The meal is accompanied by Miao folk songs and rice wine toasts. It is a festive, chaotic, and unforgettable experience.

Afternoon: The Return and Reflection

By afternoon, it is time to head back to Guiyang. The drive is long, but it gives you time to reflect on your journey. Guiyang is not a city that reveals itself easily. It is messy, humid, and sometimes frustrating. But it is also alive in a way that polished tourist destinations are not. The people are warm, the food is bold, and the landscapes are raw and powerful. Whether you spent three days or five, you will leave with a sense of having discovered something special. And you will probably be planning your return before you even reach the airport.

Practical Tips for the Guiyang Adventurer

  • Best Time to Visit: June to September for the cool weather and lush landscapes. Avoid Chinese national holidays (Golden Week in October and May Day) when domestic tourists flood the area.
  • Getting Around: Didi (the Chinese Uber) is cheap and reliable within the city. For day trips to Huangguoshu or the villages, hire a private driver for about 500-800 RMB per day. High-speed trains are also an option for longer distances.
  • Language: Mandarin is widely spoken, but English is not. Download a translation app like Pleco or Google Translate. Learn a few basic phrases like “xie xie” (thank you) and “duo shao qian” (how much).
  • Food Safety: Street food is generally safe, but stick to busy stalls with high turnover. Drink bottled water, and avoid raw vegetables unless you are sure they have been washed in purified water.
  • What to Pack: Light layers, a rain jacket, comfortable hiking shoes, insect repellent, and a reusable water bottle. The sun can be strong, so bring sunscreen and a hat.

Guiyang is waiting for you. Go get lost in the mist.
