Guiyang’s Best Spots for a Solo Traveler

  1. Home
  2. Travel Blog
  3. Blog Details

If you’ve ever dreamed of wandering through a city where misty mountains meet neon-lit streets, where the aroma of sour-and-spicy street food fills the air, and where you can hike a forest park in the morning and sip craft beer in a converted factory by night, then Guiyang is calling your name. As the capital of Guizhou Province, Guiyang has quietly become one of China’s most underrated destinations for solo travelers. It’s affordable, safe, and refreshingly free of the overwhelming crowds that plague places like Chengdu or Chongqing. The locals are warm but not pushy, the public transport is a breeze, and the climate is so mild that locals call it “the cool capital” — a perfect escape from the scorching summers elsewhere. Here’s your ultimate guide to exploring Guiyang alone, with spots that cater to every kind of solo wanderer.

Why Guiyang Works for Solo Travelers

Before diving into the spots, let’s talk about why Guiyang is a solo traveler’s dream. First, the city is compact. Most attractions are within a 30-minute ride from the city center, which means you won’t waste hours commuting. Second, the food scene is built for one. Many restaurants serve small portions or street-side snacks, so you can sample five different dishes without feeling judged for eating alone. Third, the vibe is relaxed. Guiyang doesn’t have the frantic energy of Shanghai or the tourist-trap intensity of Beijing. People here move at their own pace, and that energy is contagious. Finally, the city is a gateway to some of China’s most stunning natural wonders — Huangguoshu Waterfall, the Miao villages of Xijiang, and the surreal limestone karsts of Libo — all easily accessible by bus or high-speed rail.

Qianling Park: The Forest in the City

Start your solo adventure at Qianling Park, a sprawling green oasis that feels more like a mountain sanctuary than a city park. Located just a 15-minute bus ride from the city center, this is where locals go to escape the heat and hustle. The park is huge — over 400 hectares — and you can easily spend half a day here without covering it all. The main draw is the Qianshan Temple, a Buddhist complex dating back to the Ming Dynasty, perched on a hillside. The climb up is steep but rewarding, with sweeping views of the city skyline framed by pine trees. For solo travelers, the best part is the freedom to wander off the main path. There are countless side trails that lead to hidden pavilions, small ponds, and even a zoo (skip it unless you’re really into caged animals). Bring a book, find a quiet bench near the lake, and watch the locals practice tai chi or play mahjong. It’s a slice of slow life that’s hard to find in China’s bigger cities.

How to Get There and What to Bring

Take bus 1, 2, or 10 from the city center to the Qianling Park stop. The entrance fee is a mere 5 RMB (about 70 cents). Wear comfortable shoes — you’ll be doing a lot of walking uphill. Bring water and snacks, though there are small vendors selling grilled corn and ice cream near the temple. If you’re feeling adventurous, join the early morning crowd of seniors doing group exercises around 7 AM. They’ll probably invite you to join in, and it’s a hilarious and heartwarming way to start your day.

Jiaxiu Tower: The Iconic Postcard Shot

Every city has its landmark, and for Guiyang, it’s Jiaxiu Tower. Sitting on a stone bridge over the Nanming River, this three-story Ming Dynasty pavilion is the most photographed spot in the city. But here’s the thing — it’s not just a photo op. The tower is part of a larger cultural complex that includes a small museum, a garden, and a riverside promenade. As a solo traveler, you can take your time here. Stroll across the bridge at sunset when the tower is lit up in golden light, then wander into the adjacent Cuiwei Garden, a tranquil space with bamboo groves and koi ponds. The area is also a hub for street performers — you’ll see calligraphers painting on the ground, elderly men playing traditional instruments, and the occasional impromptu dance circle. Don’t be shy; sit on a bench and soak it in. This is the kind of place where you can people-watch for hours.

Evening Vibes Near the River

After dark, the area around Jiaxiu Tower transforms into a lively pedestrian zone. Street vendors set up stalls selling everything from grilled squid to handmade silver jewelry. Grab a skewer of spicy tofu (try the changwang mian — a local noodle soup with pig intestines and blood curd, if you’re brave) and walk along the river. The reflections of the tower and the modern bridges on the water create a dreamy atmosphere. For a solo traveler, this is the perfect low-pressure way to experience the city’s nightlife without entering a crowded bar.

Huaxi Night Market: A Feast for the Senses

No trip to Guiyang is complete without diving into its street food scene, and the Huaxi Night Market is the epicenter. Located near the Huaxi University Town, this market is less touristy and more authentic than the ones in the city center. The best time to go is around 8 PM, when the stalls are fully fired up and the crowd is buzzing. The variety is staggering — you’ll find everything from suan tang yu (sour fish soup) to si wa wa (a Guiyang-style spring roll filled with shredded vegetables and peanuts). As a solo diner, you have the advantage of being able to graze. Order a small portion of yang yu ba (mashed potato cakes fried with chili), then move to the next stall for niu rou fen (beef rice noodles in a bone broth). Don’t skip the mi dou fu — fermented tofu that’s grilled and served with a sticky, spicy sauce. It smells funky but tastes incredible.

Navigating the Market Alone

The key to enjoying Huaxi as a solo traveler is to embrace the chaos. Don’t worry about language barriers — most vendors use hand gestures and a calculator to show prices. Point at what looks good, hold up one finger, and smile. If you’re unsure about a dish, watch what the locals are eating. The market is also a great place to meet other travelers. Sit at a communal table near the grilled fish stalls, and you’ll likely strike up a conversation with a student from the nearby university or a backpacker from Europe. If you’re feeling adventurous, try the chou doufu (stinky tofu) — it’s an acquired taste, but locals swear by it.

The Guizhou Provincial Museum: A Solo Learner’s Paradise

For solo travelers who love history and culture, the Guizhou Provincial Museum is a must-visit. It’s modern, well-organized, and surprisingly uncrowded. The museum focuses on the diverse ethnic groups of Guizhou, including the Miao, Dong, and Buyi peoples. The exhibits are bilingual (Chinese and English), so you won’t feel lost. Highlights include a stunning collection of Miao silver jewelry — intricately crafted headdresses and necklaces that weigh several kilograms — and a section on the “Miao Great Wall,” a series of ancient fortifications built to control the region. There’s also a fascinating exhibit on Guizhou’s karst geology, which explains the dramatic landscapes you’ll see outside the city. As a solo traveler, you can move at your own pace. Spend an extra 20 minutes staring at the embroidery samples or skip the modern art wing if it’s not your thing. The museum is free, but you’ll need to reserve a ticket online in advance.

Practical Tips for the Museum

The museum is located in the Guanshanhu District, about a 20-minute taxi ride from the city center. Take the metro to the Guanshanhu stop and walk 10 minutes. Plan for two to three hours. The museum shop has unique souvenirs — think handwoven textiles and silver earrings — that are much better quality than what you’ll find on the street. Don’t leave without visiting the rooftop garden, which offers a panoramic view of the surrounding hills.

Qingyan Ancient Town: A Day Trip Back in Time

If you have a full day to spare, take a bus to Qingyan Ancient Town, about 30 kilometers south of Guiyang. This Ming Dynasty fortress town is less commercialized than similar spots in Yunnan or Jiangsu, and it feels genuinely lived-in. The town is built on a hillside, with stone-paved alleys winding past traditional houses, temples, and ancestral halls. As a solo traveler, you can get lost in the maze of streets without worrying about losing your group. The main attractions include the Dingguang Gate, a massive stone archway, and the Zhao Family Temple, which has beautiful wood carvings. But the real joy of Qingyan is wandering aimlessly. Duck into a small teahouse for a pot of local maojian tea, or buy a bag of qingyan tofu — a local specialty that’s smoky, chewy, and addictive.

How to Make the Most of Your Day

Take bus 210 from the Huaxi bus station to Qingyan. The ride takes about an hour. The entrance fee is 10 RMB, but you can skip the ticket by entering through one of the side alleys (locals do this all the time). Bring comfortable shoes — the cobblestones are uneven. For lunch, try the qingyan la rou (cured pork) at a family-run restaurant near the main square. The owner will probably chat with you even if you don’t speak Chinese. If you’re lucky, you’ll catch a traditional Dong song performance in the evening. The town is quiet after 6 PM, making it a peaceful end to your day.

The 798 Art District: Guiyang’s Creative Edge

Guiyang has its own version of Beijing’s 798, though it’s smaller and more intimate. Located in a former factory complex in the Yunyan District, this art district is a haven for solo travelers who appreciate contemporary art, indie coffee shops, and vintage boutiques. The galleries here feature local artists, many of whom blend traditional Miao motifs with modern techniques. The vibe is laid-back — you can spend an hour browsing a gallery, then grab a pour-over coffee at a café with mismatched furniture and exposed brick walls. The district also hosts occasional live music events and pop-up markets. As a solo traveler, this is a great place to recharge. The crowds are sparse, and the creative energy is infectious.

What to Look For

Check out the Guiyang Contemporary Art Museum within the district — it’s small but has rotating exhibits that are often thought-provoking. The courtyard has a few food trucks selling gan guo (dry pot) and leng mian (cold noodles). Don’t miss the Miao Silver Workshop on the second floor of the main building, where you can watch artisans hammering silver into intricate jewelry. If you’re feeling social, strike up a conversation with the barista at Shan Coffee — they’re usually happy to recommend other hidden spots in the city.

A Solo Hike at Qianzhong Grand Canyon

For the adventurous solo traveler, a day trip to the Qianzhong Grand Canyon is non-negotiable. Located about an hour’s drive from Guiyang, this canyon offers hiking trails that wind through lush forests, past waterfalls, and along cliffside paths. The canyon is part of a larger geological park, and the scenery is dramatic — think emerald-green rivers cutting through limestone cliffs. The main trail is about 5 kilometers and takes 3-4 hours to complete. As a solo hiker, you’ll appreciate the solitude. The trail is well-marked, and there are plenty of rest stops with benches and viewpoints. The highlight is the Tianqiao Waterfall, a 50-meter cascade that you can walk behind. The mist is refreshing, and the sound of the water is meditative.

Getting There and Staying Safe

Take a bus from the Guiyang Long-Distance Bus Station to the canyon entrance. The ride costs about 30 RMB. Alternatively, join a group tour from a hostel — many offer day trips for around 200 RMB. Bring plenty of water, sunscreen, and insect repellent. The trail can be slippery after rain, so wear hiking shoes with good grip. If you’re hiking alone, let someone know your plans. The canyon is generally safe, but it’s always smart to be prepared.

The Nightlife Scene for Solo Travelers

Guiyang’s nightlife is underrated. Unlike Shanghai or Beijing, where the party scene can feel exclusive, Guiyang’s bars and clubs are welcoming and unpretentious. The Huaguoyuan area is a good starting point — it’s a pedestrian street lined with bars, live music venues, and karaoke joints. For a solo traveler, the best bet is a place like Liquid Workshop, a craft beer bar with a rotating selection of local brews. The bartenders speak some English, and the crowd is a mix of expats and young locals. If you prefer something quieter, try The Bookworm, a cozy bar with a library vibe where you can read and sip a cocktail. The key is to be open. Guiyang’s nightlife is about connection, not spectacle.

A Note on Safety

Guiyang is one of the safest cities in China for solo travelers, even at night. The streets are well-lit, and violent crime is rare. That said, use common sense — keep your valuables close, avoid unlit alleys, and trust your instincts. The locals are friendly, but as with any city, be cautious of overly persistent strangers.

Final Thoughts on Solo Travel in Guiyang

Guiyang is a city that rewards the curious solo traveler. It’s not a place where you check off a list of attractions; it’s a place where you slow down, eat well, and let the misty mountains and friendly faces guide you. Whether you’re hiking through a canyon, bargaining for street food, or sitting alone in a temple garden, you’ll find that Guiyang has a way of making you feel both anonymous and welcome. So pack light, bring an open mind, and let the city’s cool air and warm spirit carry you through your journey.
